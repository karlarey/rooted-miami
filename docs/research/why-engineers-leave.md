# Why Relocated Tech Workers Leave Miami: Research Brief
*Hackathon track: "Brain Gain". Persona: a software engineer who moved to Miami in the 2020-22 boom and is now deciding whether to stay. Compiled 2026-10-10.*

**Main finding:** Miami is good at attracting talent and bad at keeping people who have no outside safety net. Tech headcount kept growing, but the county now loses about 70k domestic residents a year. Housing is the least affordable of any large US metro. The local tech job base is thin, so losing one job can mean leaving the city. Miami also ranks near the bottom on civic and social connection. **There is no Miami-specific, tech-only "why I left" survey.** That gap is itself an opportunity for the team.

### Flagged and could not verify
- **No Miami tech-worker departure survey or retention rate exists** in the public sources I found. LinkedIn's Miami-specific tech migration data stops at 2021. Business Insider's 2026 piece describes a two-way flow but publishes no outflow counts.
- **I found no data on remote-to-RTO departures** (remote workers forced to leave Miami when their companies called them back to the office). It is plausible but unquantified.
- **Cumulative Miami rent growth since 2020** could not be confirmed from a single index. Only the 2022 spike (about +30% YoY) and the 2025 plateau are sourced.
- **Loneliness and community evidence for tech transplants** is anecdotal (Fishbowl threads, local blogs) or not specific to tech (AmeriCorps).
- **Insurance figures:** the $5,919 premium is a broker estimate. The flood premium figure is a 2022-era projection. Check against FL Office of Insurance Regulation or FEMA NFIP data.
- **Software-developer-specific BLS wages** for Miami vs. SF/NYC were not retrieved. Only the broad occupation group is shown in #19.

## Insights for the problem statement

1. **Population numbers hide a domestic exodus.** Miami-Dade lost about 73k residents to the rest of the US in 2025 alone, and about 278k since 2020. International arrivals used to mask the loss but no longer cover it (#6-8). The fight is retention, not attraction.
2. **Housing is the top trigger to leave, and it hits hardest when someone tries to put down roots.** On the median income, 0.4% of listings are affordable (#9) and Miami has the world's top bubble-risk score (#10). Older condos, the "affordable" entry point, carry five- or six-figure assessments since Surfside (#14-16). A transplant who wants to stop renting finds there is no realistic ladder to ownership.
3. **The job market has thin depth.** Tech is a smaller share of local jobs than nationally (2.4% vs 3.4%) and pays below the US tech mean (#19). VC is about a quarter of its 2022 peak (#22-23). When the persona's remote job, startup or RTO policy changes, there may be no comparable local role. That makes one job loss a reason to move away.
4. **Miami struggles with senior talent.** It has plenty of juniors but few senior engineers, according to the Beacon Council (#24). Rabois-backed startups opened offices elsewhere to hire engineers (#22). A mid-career engineer sees a shallow ladder, and companies follow the senior talent out.
5. **Weak community is the hidden factor.** Miami ranks bottom-3 nationally on civic and neighborly ties (#27). Transplants report it is hard to make friends. Without local roots, nothing offsets cost and career pressure when the leave-or-stay decision comes.
6. **Young professionals are already halfway out.** About 52% of childless 18-34s say they are likely to leave, one of the highest rates in the country (#25). Cost, career growth, transit and climate risk are the stated reasons. The persona sits in this group.
7. **Rents have plateaued, which opens a window.** Rents are roughly flat and condo inventory is high (#13, #16). Some homeowners insurance rates are easing (#17). A retention intervention now (housing pathways, a local job-matching safety net, community onboarding) arrives when the cost of staying is no longer climbing as fast.
8. **There is a data gap the team could fill.** No public source tracks why Miami tech transplants leave (see flags). A tool that measures retention risk, such as a "stay score" or an exit survey, would be new.
